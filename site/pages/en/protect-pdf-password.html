How to protect a PDF with a password

A tax return, a medical report, a contract you send by e-mail: a password makes sure that only the person you give it to can read the file, even if the e-mail is forwarded or the attachment ends up in the wrong place.

What a password on a PDF really does

A PDF can carry two kinds of protection, and they are often confused.

  • A password to open the file. The content of every page is encrypted. Without the password, a PDF reader shows nothing at all, and the text cannot be read by opening the file in another program either. This is real protection, as strong as the password you choose.
  • Permissions, such as “no printing” or “no copying”. They are instructions that well-behaved PDF readers follow. They are useful to discourage casual copying, but they are not a lock: someone who can open the file can always take screenshots.

The editor sets both. It encrypts the file with AES-256, the strongest method the PDF standard offers, and lets you choose whether printing and copying the text are allowed.

Step by step

  1. Open the editor and open your PDF. It stays on your computer; it is not uploaded.
  2. Click Tools, then Protect with a password.
  3. Type the password twice.
  4. Choose the permissions: Allow printing is ticked by default, Allow copying the text is not.
  5. Click Protect. Chrome and Edge ask where to save the protected copy; other browsers download it.

Open the new file once to check: your PDF reader should ask for the password before it shows anything.

Choosing the password

Encryption is only as good as the password. A short word or a date of birth can be guessed by a program that tries millions of combinations, and a PDF that has left your hands can be attacked offline for as long as someone likes.

  • Prefer a phrase of four or five unrelated words, such as lamp river orange seventeen, over a short word full of symbols. It is longer, so harder to guess, and easier to dictate over the phone.
  • Use a different password for each recipient or each document, never one of your account passwords.
  • The editor needs at least 4 characters and refuses commas: this is a technical limit of the PDF tool behind it, not a rule about strength.

Sharing the password safely

Never send the password in the same e-mail as the PDF: anyone who gets the e-mail would get both. Send it another way: a text message, a phone call, or a message app. For documents you send regularly to the same person, agree on a password once, in person or by phone.

Opening, editing and removing the password

When you open a protected PDF in the editor, it asks for the password, then shows the document normally. The copy you save from there is not protected any more: this is how you remove a password from your own file. If you edit a protected PDF and send it again, use Protect with a password once more before you share it.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Forgetting the password. Nobody can recover it, not even us: your file never reaches our servers, and AES-256 cannot be undone without the password. Keep the original, unprotected file in a safe place, or store the password in a password manager.
  • Relying on “no copying” alone. If the content is secret, the password to open is what protects it. Permissions only make copying less convenient.
  • Protecting a file that still contains what you meant to remove. A password protects the whole file from outsiders, not parts of it from the recipient. To send a document with some details removed, redact it first.
  • Very old PDF readers. AES-256 is read by Adobe Acrobat Reader, Apple Preview and the PDF viewers built into Chrome, Edge and Firefox. Software older than about 2008 may refuse the file.
